How a tap becomes a chart in under a minute

Two billion events a day pass through the same seven stops. This is the path one of them takes, from a thumb on a screen to a line on a dashboard, and why each stop is there.

PRTE Priya Raman and Tom Ellery · 14 October 2026
Follow one event
Stop 1 · Sources

Every tap is an event

Our SDKs don't send a request per tap. The mobile SDK batches events for up to five seconds or fifty events, whichever comes first, and the web app hands its batch to the browser's beacon API so it survives the tab closing.

Each event carries an ID minted on the device. That ID is what lets every later stop drop duplicates without coordinating.

2.1 B events a day
Stop 2 · Collector

The collector takes them in

The collector is deliberately dumb. It checks the schema version, stamps a server time and writes the batch to the stream. It never calls a database, so a slow warehouse can't make an app wait.

12k events per second at peak
Stop 3 · Stream

The stream keeps them in order

Events are partitioned by account, so everything one customer does stays in order. The stream holds seven days, which is our undo button: any consumer can rewind and replay.

7 days of replay
Stop 4 · Processors

Processors clean, join and enrich

Three jobs read the stream: one drops duplicates by event ID, one joins events to accounts and plans, and one turns IP addresses into countries before throwing the IPs away.

Each job commits its read position only after its writes land, so a crash replays a few seconds instead of losing them.

Stop 5 · Lake

The raw events go to the lake, untouched

A second consumer copies the stream, byte for byte, into cheap object storage, partitioned by hour. When a processor has a bug, we fix it and rebuild from the lake instead of asking customers to resend anything.

0 backfills that needed a customer
Stop 6 · Warehouse

Tables land in the warehouse

Clean events are appended to wide, columnar tables every ten seconds. Click the warehouse in the diagram to open it: raw events at the bottom, sessions in the middle, and the daily rollups that most charts actually read at the top.

Stop 7 · Dashboard

And the chart moves

Dashboards query the rollups, so a chart over a year of data reads a few thousand rows, not a few billion. From the tap to the line moving, the 95th percentile is forty-eight seconds.

48 s tap to chart, p95

What we'd do differently

We would have built the lake first. For two years the stream was the only copy of raw events, and seven days of replay felt like plenty until the bug we found on day nine.

The cheapest copy of your data is the one that lets you be wrong.
